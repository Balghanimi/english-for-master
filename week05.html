Connection to Week 4: Week 4 focused on precise technical description: exact nouns, measurable values, objective wording, and suitable tense. Week 5 transfers those skills to graphs, charts, and figures.
Why this matters for your research: Describing data is the core skill of the Results chapter of a thesis and of every journal paper. Examiners and reviewers read your figures and your sentences together; imprecise numbers, vague adjectives, or claims the data cannot support are among the most common reasons for revision requests. Bring one figure from your own research (or pilot data) to this session if you have one.

Warm-up 8 min

Diagnose a Weak Results Paragraph

Read before you learn the rules: what would a supervisor or reviewer circle?

Situation: An MSc student describes Figure A (PV output, 08:00–18:00, see below) in a draft Results chapter.

Figure 3 is about the PV output. The output increased a lot in the morning, reached a very high peak, and then dropped dramatically. This shows that the system is reliable and that the weather became cloudy in the afternoon.

  1. Individually (3 min): underline every vague word, every unsupported claim, and everything missing (values, units, time).
  2. In pairs (3 min): rewrite the first two sentences using only information you can see in Figure A.
  3. Class (2 min): compare, then open the model.
Show model diagnosis
  • Vague: “is about”, “a lot”, “very high”, “dramatically”: no values, no units, no times.
  • Unsupported cause: “the weather became cloudy”: not shown by the figure.
  • Unsupported evaluation: “the system is reliable”: one day of data cannot show reliability.
  • Missing: time span, peak value and time, final value.

Possible rewrite: Fig. 3 shows PV output between 08:00 and 18:00. Output rose from 0.8 kW at 08:00 to a peak of 5.4 kW at 14:00 and then fell to 0.7 kW by 18:00.

Course framework

Describe → Analyse → Evaluate

The original graph materials use three stages. Week 5 concentrates on the first two; Week 6 develops evaluation.

1. DESCRIBE THE LAYOUTWhat is the title? What do the axes, units, legend, and time period show?
2. ANALYSE VISIBLE PATTERNSWhat increased, decreased, peaked, remained stable, or differed?
3. EVALUATEWhy might it happen? What are the implications? What may happen next? Week 6.
Week 5 boundary: analyse what is visible in the data, but do not invent causes. “Output declined after 14:00” is supported by the figure. “Cloud cover caused the decline” needs external evidence.

Use different words when they fit the data. Do not replace a word only to avoid repetition.

Meaning controls vocabulary. Use surge, soar, plunge, or plummet only for clearly large and rapid changes. Use significant only when you have run a statistical test and can report it (e.g., p < 0.05); otherwise choose substantial, marked, or give the value. Tie strength words to numbers: “a marked increase (from 12 to 31 mW)”.
Strength and speed

Adjectives and Adverbs for Trends

Small changeslight / slightly
modest / modestly
Slow, continuous changegradual / gradually
steady / steadily
Large changemarked / markedly

Fast changesharp / sharply

rapid / rapidly
Alternatives for the same meaning. sharply → steeply, rapidly, dramatically; gradually → steadily, slowly, progressively; slightly → marginally, modestly. Dramatically is a strong word: use it only for a very large change, and give the numbers. Significantly still needs a statistical test.
Collocation: a sharp increase / increased sharply; a gradual decline / declined gradually.

Flexible academic writing Optional · pre-class

One Trend, Many Ways to Write It

The same data can be reported with different grammar. Learn several structures so that your Results paragraphs do not repeat one pattern.

Four structuresVerb + adverb: Output rose sharply…Adjective + noun: There was a sharp rise in…Noun-phrase subject: A sharp rise in … was recorded…“Saw”: The period … saw a sharp rise…
StructureEquivalent academic sentence (values from this lesson’s figures)
1. Sharp rise — Figure A, 08:00–12:00
Verb + adverbOutput rose sharply from 0.8 kW at 08:00 to 4.9 kW at 12:00.
Adjective + nounThere was a sharp rise in output, from 0.8 to 4.9 kW, between 08:00 and 12:00.
Noun-phrase subjectA sharp rise in output, from 0.8 to 4.9 kW, was recorded between 08:00 and 12:00.
“Saw” structureThe period 08:00–12:00 saw a sharp rise in output, from 0.8 to 4.9 kW.
Hedged / approximateOutput rose by just over 4 kW, from roughly 0.8 kW to nearly 5 kW, during the morning.
2. Gradual fall — Table 2, gap between X and Y, 20–80% load
Verb + adverbThe efficiency gap between the two topologies fell gradually from 4 percentage points at 20% load to 1 percentage point at 80% load.
Adjective + nounThere was a gradual fall in the efficiency gap, from 4 to 1 percentage point, as load rose from 20% to 80%.
Noun-phrase subjectA gradual fall in the efficiency gap, from 4 to 1 percentage point, was observed between 20% and 80% load.
“Saw” structureThe 20–80% load range saw a gradual fall in the efficiency gap, from 4 to 1 percentage point.
Hedged / approximateThe efficiency gap tended to fall gradually, from 4 percentage points to about 1 percentage point.
3. Peak — Figure A, 14:00
Verb (+ adverb)Output peaked at 5.4 kW at 14:00 before declining sharply.
Adjective + nounOutput reached a peak of 5.4 kW at 14:00.
Noun-phrase subjectA peak output of 5.4 kW was recorded at 14:00.
“Saw” structure14:00 saw the highest output of the day, at 5.4 kW.
Hedged / approximateOutput peaked at approximately 5.4 kW at about 14:00 (read from the plot).
4. Stability / plateau — Voltage, rated 230 V
Verb + adverbVoltage remained stable at 230 V throughout the measurement period.
Adjective + nounThere was minimal variation in voltage, which stayed at 230 V.
Noun-phrase subjectA stable voltage of 230 V was maintained throughout the measurement period.
“Saw” structureThe measurement period saw voltage remain stable at 230 V.
Hedged / approximateVoltage remained within ±2% of the rated 230 V.
5. Fluctuation — Frequency: 49.9 → 50.1 → 49.8 → 50.0 Hz
Verb + adverbFrequency fluctuated slightly between 49.8 and 50.1 Hz.
Adjective + nounThere were slight fluctuations in frequency between 49.8 and 50.1 Hz.
Noun-phrase subjectSlight fluctuations in frequency, between 49.8 and 50.1 Hz, were observed.
“Saw” structureThe measurement period saw frequency fluctuate slightly between 49.8 and 50.1 Hz.
Hedged / approximateFrequency remained approximately stable around 50 Hz, varying by no more than ±0.2 Hz.
6. Comparison of two values — Figure B, System C 145 Wh and System B 95 Wh
ComparativeSystem C’s loss (145 Wh) was considerably higher than that of System B (95 Wh).
Contrast linkerSystem C recorded a loss of 145 Wh, whereas System B recorded 95 Wh.
Noun-phrase subjectA difference of 50 Wh in energy loss was recorded between System C and System B.
“Experienced” structureSystem C experienced a loss 50 Wh higher than System B.
Hedged / approximateSystem C’s loss was about 53% higher than that of System B (145 compared with 95 Wh).
7. Comparison of shares — Figure C, solar 35% and wind 15%
ComparativeSolar’s share (35%) was more than twice as high as wind’s (15%).
Contrast linkerSolar accounted for 35% of the supply, compared with 15% for wind.
Noun-phrase subjectA gap of 20 percentage points was recorded between the shares of solar and wind.
Hedged / approximateSolar’s share was roughly 2.3 times that of wind.

Results chapter

Use the full range. Vary the structure from sentence to sentence, and give exact values and times. Noun-phrase subjects (was recorded, was observed) and “saw” structures suit a longer description of a figure. Use a hedged form when a value is read from a plot.

Abstract

Choose the shortest accurate form: verb + adverb with one key value (Output rose sharply from 0.8 to 4.9 kW). Avoid “saw” structures, long noun phrases and repeated hedging, because every word counts.

Equivalent does not mean exchangeable everywhere. Keep the same values and the same strength of claim in every version. Considerably and sharply still need numbers, and significantly is still reserved for a statistical test.

Grammar of data

From / To / By / At / Between

Small prepositions can change the numerical meaning.

FunctionPatternExample
Start and end valuesX rose from A to B.Efficiency rose from 82% to 96%.
Amount of changeX rose by N.Power increased by 2.4 kW.
Final valueX rose to B.Output climbed to 5.4 kW.
Stable valueX remained stable at A.Voltage remained stable at 230 V.
RangeX fluctuated between A and B.Frequency varied between 49.9 and 50.1 Hz.
Maximum / minimumX peaked at A / reached a peak of A.Output peaked at 5.4 kW.
Noun formsan increase in X of N / from A to BThere was an increase in output of 2.4 kW. (in = what changed; of = how much)

BY is not TO

“The value decreased by 20%” = the amount of change is 20%.

“The value decreased to 20%” = the final value is 20%.

Percent vs percentage points

Efficiency: 82% → 96%

Difference: 14 percentage points.

Relative increase: about 17.1%.

(96 − 82) / 82 × 100 ≈ 17.1%
Engineering quantities

Reporting Change Precisely

Research readers expect the type of change to be unambiguous.

IdeaPatternExample
Absolute changeincreased by N unitLosses increased by 25 Wh.
Relative changeincreased by N% (state the base)Losses increased by 26% relative to System B (95 → 120 Wh).
Percentage pointsby N percentage points (pp)Efficiency rose by 14 percentage points (82% → 96%).
Multiplesdoubled / halved / threefold / by a factor of NSwitching loss increased by a factor of 3 (from 12 to 36 mW).
ProportionN out of M / about one third ofSolar supplied about one third of the demand.
Orders of magnitudediffer by N orders of magnitudeThe leakage current was three orders of magnitude lower than the load current.
Tolerance / spreadX ± N unit; within ±N% ofVoltage remained within ±2% of the rated 230 V.
Values read from a plotapproximately / roughly / just over / just under / nearlyOutput peaked at approximately 5.4 kW (read from the plot).
Trap: “increase of 300%”. A 300% increase from 10 W gives 40 W (four times the original), not 30 W. When in doubt, write the multiple (“four times”, “a factor of 4”) or both values (“from 10 W to 40 W”). Give the base for every relative change, and keep the precision of a value no higher than the instrument or the plot allows.
Grammar of reporting

Tense and Reporting Verbs

Tense signals whether you are describing the document, your own results, or the literature.

Present Simple

Use it to refer to a figure or table in the current document, and for established facts.

Figure 4 shows a gradual increase in efficiency.

The bar chart compares three systems.

Past Simple

Use it for your own completed measurements and for specific historical data.

Efficiency increased from 82% to 96% during the test.

Electricity demand fell in 2020.

Present Perfect

Use it for developments that continue to the present, especially in the literature or national statistics.

Installed PV capacity has grown since 2015.

Smith et al. [4] reported that losses fell by 8% (past: a single study).

Keep tense consistent within a paragraph unless the time reference genuinely changes. Interpretation (Week 6) usually returns to the present: “This suggests that…”.

Accuracy clinic

Common Errors in Graph Writing

Academic vocabulary does not compensate for inaccurate data.

ErrorWeak / incorrectBetter
Wrong unitOutput reached 5.4 W.Output reached 5.4 kW.
Wrong directionVoltage increased from 230 V to 210 V.Voltage decreased from 230 V to 210 V.
Percent vs pointEfficiency increased by 14% (82 → 96).Efficiency increased by 14 percentage points.
Unsupported causePV output fell because of clouds.PV output fell from 5.4 kW to 0.7 kW after 14:00.
OverstatementA dramatic change occurred.The value increased by 6%.
False precisionFrequency was 49.987436 Hz from a coarse graph.Frequency was approximately 50.0 Hz.
Wrong comparisonA is higher from B.A is higher than B.
Unclear pronounIt increased sharply.Output increased sharply.
“Significant” without a testThere was a significant increase in losses.Losses increased substantially, from 12 to 31 mW.
Ambiguous percentagePower increased by 300% (10 → 30 W).Power increased by 200%, from 10 to 30 W.
Evaluation without evidenceThe results prove the converter is reliable.Efficiency exceeded 93% at all five tested loads.

Frequent EAL errors in data writing

  • rise / raise: “Output rose” (no object) but “The controller raised the output”.
  • “higher from B” → higher than B.
  • “an increase of demand” → an increase in demand.
  • “the Figure 3 shows” → Figure 3 shows (no article before a numbered figure).
  • “15 percents” → 15 percent / 15%.
  • number with countable nouns (the number of samples), amount with uncountable (the amount of energy).
  • data: use are/show in formal engineering writing, or follow the journal you target; stay consistent.

Using AI tools responsibly

AI writing assistants can polish grammar and suggest alternative verbs, but they may change numbers, units or direction without warning, and may add causes that your data do not support.

  • Check every value, unit and direction against your figure after any edit.
  • Do not paste unpublished data into public tools if your supervisor or funder restricts it.
  • Follow your university and target-journal policy on declaring AI assistance.

Paragraph structure

Build a Graph Paragraph in Four Moves

1. Introduce

State what the figure shows, including period or categories.

2. Overview

State the main pattern without too many numbers.

3. Key details

Support the overview with selected values.

4. Compare

Highlight one meaningful difference, high/low point, or proportion.

Weak paragraph

The graph is very interesting. PV output went up a lot and then came down. It was high at 14:00 and low later. This probably happened because the weather changed. The graph has many changes.

Improved academic paragraph

The line graph shows PV output between 08:00 and 18:00. Overall, output rose throughout the morning, peaked in the early afternoon, and then declined. It increased from 0.8 kW at 08:00 to 5.4 kW at 14:00. Thereafter, output fell sharply, reaching 0.7 kW by 18:00. The afternoon decline was larger than the increase recorded between 08:00 and 10:00.

Thesis and paper conventions

Captions and Referring to Figures

A figure needs three things: a self-contained caption, a reference in the text, and a description of what matters.

IEEEAPA 7
Figure label and captionBelow the figure: “Fig. 1. Caption in sentence case.”Above the figure: Figure 1 on one line, then the title in italic Title Case on the next line
Table label and captionAbove the table: “TABLE I” and a title in capitalsAbove the table, same format as a figure (bold number, italic title)
In the text“as shown in Fig. 1”; “Figs. 1 and 2”; “Table I” (Fig. is abbreviated; Table is not)“Figure 1 shows…”; “Table 1 presents…” (never abbreviated)
Source or noteCite in the caption: “…data from [3].”A Note. line below, with the source and permission if reproduced
Axis labelsQuantity and unit: “Power (kW)”Same: clear label with unit
  • Mention every figure in the text before it appears, and never leave a figure unexplained.
  • A caption should be understandable on its own: variable, condition, period, and (for a series) what each line shows.
  • Check your department's thesis template: its rules override general style guides.

  • Truncated axis: starting at 91% makes Q's bar about three times taller than P's (3.3 vs 1.1 units), although the relative difference is only about 2.4%.
  • Overstatement: “dramatically” is not supported by a difference of 2.2 percentage points.
  • Missing uncertainty and conditions: without repeats or error bars, and without the test load, the reader cannot judge whether the difference is meaningful.

Rewrite: System Q recorded a higher efficiency than System P (94.3% and 92.1%, respectively), a difference of 2.2 percentage points; the figure does not report repeat measurements, so the reliability of this difference cannot be assessed.

Checklist: at least three problems · exact values · percentage points · no “significant” or “dramatic” · limitation stated.

Describe a Figure from Your Own Research

Build a piece you can reuse in your thesis or paper. Due before Week 6.

Choose a figure from your own research (experimental, simulation, or pilot data). If you have no data yet, use a figure from a key paper in your field, or the Iraq Energy Profile.

  1. Caption (IEEE or APA 7, as your department requires): self-contained, with variables, units, and conditions.
  2. Results paragraph, 150–200 words: in-text reference, opening sentence, overview, at least five exact values with units, and at least one comparison.
  3. Use at least four different, accurate trend or comparison expressions, two noun forms, two verb forms, and one adjective/adverb pair. Report relative change and percentage points correctly.
  4. Include one sentence on a limitation of the figure (scale, uncertainty, conditions, short period). Do not explain causes or evaluate yet.
  5. Revision log (80–100 words): paste your first draft sentence and your revised sentence for three changes, and say which rubric criterion each change improves.
  6. If you used an AI tool for language support, add one line saying how, and confirm that you checked every number and unit yourself.

Submit: the figure (or its link), caption, paragraph, revision log, and the peer-review score sheet.
