Connection to Week 5: Week 5 asked “What does the data show?” and trained you to read figures, select values and describe trends, comparisons and quantities accurately, without inventing causes. Week 6 asks “What does the pattern mean, and how far can we trust it?” You will add comparison across conditions, interpretation, evidence from the literature, implications and limitations, all with claims of the right strength.
Why this matters for your research: In a thesis, the Results chapter reports what you found and the Discussion chapter explains what it means. Examiners may comment that claims are “not supported by the data” or that “no limitations are discussed”. Both problems are language problems as much as science problems. Bring the figure, caption, paragraph and revision log from your Week 5 homework: you will upgrade that paragraph in the homework.

Warm-up + Activity A 12 min

Describing Is Not Yet Analysing

Read a Week 5-style paragraph, decide what is still missing for a Discussion chapter, then rewrite it as an analysis.

Situation: An MSc student describes Fig. 1 (efficiency of two converter designs; the data are in the Figures section) in a draft Results chapter.

Fig. 1 shows the efficiency of Design A and Design B at six loads. Design A was 91.0% at 10% load and 95.1% at 100% load. Design B was 86.5% at 10% load and 96.3% at 100% load. Design A was higher at low loads and Design B was higher at high loads.

Placeholder references: [3], [5] and [7] in this lesson are hypothetical placeholders for classroom practice. They are not real papers and their values are not real findings.

ScaffoldStep 1 · Diagnose 3 min

The description is accurate. Individually (2 min), list four things a supervisor would still ask (for example: So what? Where do they cross? Why? How sure are you?), then compare in pairs (1 min).

Show model diagnosis
  • No quantified comparison: “higher” has no size (4.5 percentage points at 10% load; 1.2 percentage points at 100% load).
  • No crossover: the reader is not told where the order reverses (between 60% and 80% load, about 66% by linear interpolation).
  • No interpretation: what does the pattern imply for use? (e.g., This may suggest that Design B is better suited to high-load operation.)
  • No explanation, literature link or limitation: a reason, a comparison with [3], and a statement of what was tested (one prototype per design).

ScaffoldStep 2 · Activity A: upgrade the paragraph 7 min + 2 min comparison

Task: Rewrite the paragraph as 4–5 sentences that add (1) a quantified comparison, (2) the crossover, (3) a calibrated interpretation, (4) a possible reason or implication, and (5) a limitation. Keep Results sentences in the past and interpretation in the present.

Show model answer and checklist

Fig. 1 compares the efficiency of two converter designs at six loads. Design A was more efficient at low load, by 4.5 percentage points at 10% load, whereas Design B was more efficient at 80% and 100% load, by 0.7 and 1.2 percentage points; the curves crossed between 60% and 80% load (about 66% by linear interpolation). This may suggest that Design B is better suited to converters operating mainly at high load, and a possible explanation is lower switching loss at high current. However, only one prototype of each design was tested, so the difference at high load should be interpreted with caution.

  • Comparison quantified in percentage points with load levels given.
  • Crossover located (between 60% and 80% load).
  • Interpretation uses a calibrated verb (may suggest: one prototype, no repeats); the reason is hedged (a possible explanation is).
  • A specific limitation is stated; tense moves from past to present.
Results versus Discussion

Six Moves from Observation to Limitation

The same figure is used twice: first to report what was found, then to say what it means.

Results versus Discussion
FeatureResultsDiscussion
Question answeredWhat did we find?What does it mean, and how far can we trust it?
Typical contentValues, trends, comparisons, figure and table referencesInterpretation, explanations, literature comparison, implications, limitations
Main tensePast simple for your own results (Design B reached 96.7% at 80% load); present simple to refer to a figure (Fig. 1 shows that the curves cross).Present simple with a hedge for interpretation (This may suggest…) and for general truths (Switching loss increases with switching frequency); a modal for implications (Design B could reduce losses); past simple for one cited study (Reference [3] reported 96.2%) and present perfect for a body of work (Several studies have reported lower losses with soft switching [3], [5]); past for what you did and present for its consequence (Only three repeats were performed, so the result should be interpreted with caution).
CitationsRarely, except for methods or data sourcesOften: to compare, to support an explanation, to show agreement or difference
Claim strengthClose to the data: verbs of observation (was, reached, was lower than); no explanation unless testedCalibrated to the evidence: may suggest, may be attributed to, is consistent with

Some theses combine both in one chapter (“Results and Discussion”). The moves stay the same; the tense changes as you move from reporting to interpreting.

“Outperformed” is evaluative. It says that one option is better, not only that its value is lower. Use it only when the difference is clear against the error bars; otherwise write was lower than.

“Times higher” is ambiguous

“1.7 times higher than” can be read as 1.7 or as 2.7 times the original. Prefer 1.7 times as high as, 1.7 times that of, or give both values (148 and 87 Wh). Report the base of every percentage: “41% lower than Method 1”.

Compare like with like

“Method 3’s loss was lower than Method 1.” (compares a loss with a method). Better: “Method 3’s loss was lower than that of Method 1.” Use compared with or relative to, and keep the same unit on both sides.

Calibrated claims

How Strong Is Your Claim?

Match the verb to what you assert and to the evidence behind it. Overclaiming and underclaiming are both errors.

Strength bands (classroom convention)1 Observed fact › 2 Supported inference › 3 Tentative or untested explanation
1 · Observed factWhat the data directly show. Use verbs of observation: was, reached, was lower than, fell; Fig. 1 shows…. No hedge is needed.
Output at 12:00 was 4.7 ± 0.3 kW, against 5.0 kW simulated (Fig. 2).
2 · Supported inferenceA statement about meaning that the evidence supports: repeats, a consistent pattern or agreement with an independent source. Verbs: suggests, indicates, is consistent with.
The close agreement at five of six times indicates that the model predicts output well at those times (n = 5 days).
3 · Tentative or untested explanationA reading of limited evidence (a single run, no repeats) or a reason that was not tested. Verbs: may, could, may suggest, may indicate, may be attributed to, a possible explanation is.
This may suggest that Design B is better suited to high load; only one prototype of each design was tested.

How to use the bands

  • Decide first what you assert, then check that the evidence can carry that band.
  • One prototype and no repeats (Fig. 1): the observation stays in band 1, but any interpretation of it stays in band 3 (may suggest, may indicate).
  • Underclaiming is also an error: a value that was measured is band 1. Design B might possibly have been a little more efficient at 100% load hedges a number that is in the table.

A classroom convention

The three bands are a convention for this course. Journals, supervisors and fields differ, and verbs such as suggests and indicates overlap in published writing. Use the bands to decide how much to claim, then follow the practice of your supervisor and your target journal.

Avoid: proves, definitely, obviously, it is clear that, undoubtedly unless the evidence is mathematical, and treat demonstrates and confirms with care: they need controlled, repeated or independently verified evidence. Hedging devices: modal verbs (may, could), lexical verbs (suggest, appear, tend), adverbs (possibly, probably, partly). This week you need hedging only for interpretation; Week 10 (Fact, Opinion, Evidence and Academic Neutrality) develops fact versus opinion and hedging in depth.
Correlation, causation and “significant”

Do Not Claim More Than the Design Allows

Causal language needs a causal design

  • Non-causal (safe): was associated with · coincided with · occurred together with · was accompanied by.
  • Causal (needs control): caused · led to · resulted in · was due to · reduced.
  • Fig. 2: low output and a large SD occurred together at 14:00. Say that they occurred together; do not state a cause without irradiance data (Activity E shows how to word an untested reason).
  • Fig. 3: if all three methods ran on the same prototype under the same load profile, and the order of the tests was randomised, “Method 3 reduced the loss” is a reasonable causal claim. If conditions differed, write “was associated with a lower loss”.

When “significant” is allowed

  • Only after a statistical test, reported with the test, the p value and n: “…was statistically significant (p < 0.05, n = 5).”
  • No test was run for Figs. 1–3, describe a large gap with an adverb such as substantially or markedly, or simply give the value.
  • Overlapping SD bars (Method 2: 94 ± 7; Method 3: 87 ± 6 Wh) do not resolve the 7 Wh difference. An overlap is not a significance test; with only three repeats, these data cannot tell the two methods apart.
  • Practical importance is separate from statistical significance: a 0.3 pp gap may be real but small.
Evaluating a figure and data quality

Ask What the Data Can Support

A good Discussion tells the reader how much weight each result can bear.

Checks for evaluating a figure
CheckQuestionLanguage frame (values from Figs. 1–3)
Sample size / repeatsHow many repeats? One prototype?Each bar is the mean of three repeated tests; with n = 3 the estimate of variability is itself uncertain. Fig. 1 reports a single prototype of each design.
Error barsWhat do they show (SD, SE, range)? Do they overlap?The SD bars of Methods 2 and 3 overlap (94 ± 7 and 87 ± 6 Wh), so they do not resolve the 7 Wh difference; an overlap is not a significance test.

Limitation formulas

  • A limitation of this study is that …
  • The results should be interpreted with caution because …
  • It was not possible to confirm whether … because …
  • Future work should … (to remove the limitation)

Good limitation vs weak limitation

Weak: “The experiment had some errors.”

Specific: “Only three repeats were performed per method, so the 7 Wh difference between Methods 2 and 3 should be interpreted with caution.”

One agreement rule, used throughout. Compare the difference between the two values with the uncertainty stated for your own value (for example 1 SD, in the same unit). If the difference is smaller, write consistent with; if it is larger, write differs from; if your value has no stated uncertainty (as in Fig. 1), say so and write similar to. This is a classroom rule of thumb, not a statistical test, and it does not replace one.

IEEE in-text citation

  • Numbered, in square brackets, in order of first appearance: [3]; several sources: [3], [5]; a range: [3]–[5].
  • Place the number before the punctuation: “…reported in [3].”
  • Begin a sentence with In [3], or Reference [3] reported; use the full name only when it adds meaning.
  • Give the full reference in the reference list. In class we use [3], [5] and [7] as hypothetical placeholders only.

A fair comparison checklist

  • Same quantity and unit (efficiency at rated load, not peak against rated).
  • Similar conditions or rating? If not, say so.
  • Report both values and the difference.
  • Give a possible reason for any difference, and hedge it.
  • Do not write “confirms” for a single close value; write is consistent with or is similar to.

Notes on register

  • Impersonal passives (It may be inferred that…) are formal and common in theses; avoid stacking them.
  • Noun-phrase subjects (The efficiency advantage of Design B emerged…) suit a Discussion; verb forms suit an Abstract.
  • Keep the same values in every version. If you change the strength band, you have changed the claim.

OptionalQuick Practice — Three Ways Optional · pre-class · 5 min

Situation (Fig. 3): Method 3 recorded 87 ± 6 Wh and Method 2 recorded 94 ± 7 Wh. Write the same limitation in three different structures, without using “significant”. Keep the values exact.

Show model answer
  • “do not resolve”: The 7 Wh difference between Method 3 (87 ± 6 Wh) and Method 2 (94 ± 7 Wh) is not resolved by the data, because the SD bars overlap.
  • “however” + caution: Method 3 recorded a lower loss than Method 2 (by 7 Wh, or about 7%); however, the overlapping SD bars mean that this result should be interpreted with caution.

Part 2 · Critique (7 min).

Fig. 2 shows that the simulation is wrong because the output at 14:00 was much lower. The significant difference proves that clouds reduced the output. This is a big problem and the model is bad. Our results are better than all previous studies. The system will work perfectly in all conditions.

Task: (1) Identify at least three problems and name each (overclaim, causal error, untested “significant”, vague wording, unsupported literature claim, missing limitation). (2) Rewrite the first two sentences using the values of Fig. 2.

Show model answer and checklist

Part 1: (i) 4 · (ii) 1 · (iii) 6 · (iv) 3 · (v) 5 · (vi) 2.

  • Faulty inference: a single anomalous time does not show that “the simulation is wrong”.
  • Vague: “much lower”, “a big problem”, “bad” give no values (2.8 against 4.0 kW).
  • Untested “significant”: no test was run; the SD at 14:00 is large.
  • Unsupported cause: “proves that clouds reduced” with no irradiance data.
  • Unsupported literature claim: “better than all previous studies” has no citation and no comparison.
  • Overclaim: “will work perfectly in all conditions”.
  • Missing limitation.

Rewrite of the first two sentences: At 14:00, mean measured output (2.8 ± 0.9 kW) was 1.2 kW, or 30%, below the simulated 4.0 kW, whereas the two agreed closely at the other five times (Fig. 2). This may be attributed to intermittent cloud cover, but irradiance was not recorded, so the explanation remains untested.

Extension (full paragraph): add that the model appears to predict output well at most times and that the 14:00 discrepancy should be investigated with logged irradiance data.

Checklist: at least three problems named · values and units · calibrated verbs · no “significant” or “proves” · limitation stated.

Daily loss was 148 ± 6 Wh for Method 1, compared with 94 ± 7 Wh for Method 2 and 87 ± 6 Wh for Method 3 (Fig. 3). Method 3 had the lowest loss, 41% below Method 1, but its 7 Wh advantage over Method 2 lies within the error bars. Show model answer and checklist

The 36% lower loss of Method 2 relative to Method 1 (148 to 94 Wh) is slightly smaller than the 38% reported in [5]; the difference of 2 percentage points is about 3 Wh on a 148 Wh base, smaller than the SDs in Fig. 3 (6 and 7 Wh), so the values are consistent with each other, although [5] reports no uncertainty. The difference may reflect differences in load profile or component ratings, which cannot be checked from [5].

  • Both values and their difference are given; the base of the percentage is stated.
  • The difference is compared with the uncertainty; citation in IEEE style, number before punctuation.
