Connection to Week 5: Week 5 asked “What does the data show?” and trained you to read figures, select values and describe trends, comparisons and quantities accurately, without inventing causes. Week 6 asks “What does the pattern mean, and how far can we trust it?” You will add comparison across conditions, interpretation, evidence from the literature, implications and limitations, all with claims of the right strength.
Why this matters for your research: In a thesis, the Results chapter reports what you found and the Discussion chapter explains what it means. Examiners often write “claims not supported by the data” or “no limitations discussed”. Both problems are language problems as much as science problems. Bring the figure, caption, paragraph and revision log from your Week 5 homework: you will upgrade that paragraph today and in the homework.
Memory rule

Show → Compare → Interpret → Qualify

Analysis is a sequence of moves. Each move has its own language, tense and claim strength.

SHOWReport the key values accurately (Week 5 skills).
COMPAREContrast series or conditions; quantify the gap; note crossovers.
INTERPRETSay what it suggests; offer a reason; link to literature.
QUALIFYState implications, limitations and the strength of each claim.

Warm-up 8 min

Describing Is Not Yet Analysing

Read a Week 5-style paragraph: what is still missing for a Discussion chapter?

Situation: An MSc student describes Fig. 1 (efficiency of two converter designs, below) in a draft Results chapter.

Fig. 1 shows the efficiency of Design A and Design B at six loads. Design A was 91.0% at 10% load and 95.1% at 100% load. Design B was 86.5% at 10% load and 96.3% at 100% load. Design A was higher at low loads and Design B was higher at high loads.

  1. Individually (3 min): the description is accurate. List four things a supervisor would still ask (for example: So what? Where do they cross? Why? How sure are you?).
  2. In pairs (3 min): add one sentence that interprets the pattern, using a verb that does not overclaim.
  3. Class (2 min): compare, then open the model.
Show model diagnosis
  • No quantified comparison: “higher” has no size (4.5 percentage points at 10% load; 1.2 percentage points at 100% load).
  • No crossover: the reader is not told where the order reverses (approximately 65% load).
  • No interpretation: what does the pattern imply for use? (e.g., This suggests that Design B is better suited to high-load operation.)
  • No explanation, literature link or limitation: a reason, a comparison with [3], and a statement of what was tested (one prototype per design).

Possible added sentence: This suggests that Design B is better suited to converters that operate mainly at high load.

Results versus Discussion

Six Moves from Observation to Limitation

The same figure is used twice: first to report what was found, then to say what it means.

ResultsDiscussion
Question answeredWhat did we find?What does it mean, and how far can we trust it?
Typical contentValues, trends, comparisons, figure and table referencesInterpretation, explanations, literature comparison, implications, limitations
Main tensePast for your measurements; present to refer to a figure (“Fig. 1 shows…”)Present for interpretation and general truths; past for what you or others did or found
CitationsRarely, except for methods or data sourcesOften: to compare, to support an explanation, to show agreement or difference
Claim strengthClose to the data; no explanation unless testedCalibrated: suggests, may be attributed to, is consistent with

Some theses combine both in one chapter (“Results and Discussion”). The moves stay the same; the tense changes as you move from reporting to interpreting.

Six moves1 Observation2 Comparison3 Interpretation4 Explanation with evidence5 Implication6 Limitation
MoveModel sentence for Fig. 1 (values from this lesson’s figures)
Results — past tense, close to the data
1 ObservationBoth designs exceeded 95% efficiency between 40% and 100% load, peaking at 96.4% (Design A, 60% load) and 96.7% (Design B, 80% load) (Fig. 1).
2 ComparisonDesign A was more efficient at low load, by 4.5 percentage points at 10% load, whereas Design B was more efficient above approximately 65% load, with a gap of 1.2 percentage points at 100% load.
Discussion — present tense for interpretation; calibrated claims
3 InterpretationThis suggests that Design B is better suited to applications in which the converter operates mainly at high load.
4 Explanation with evidenceA possible explanation is lower switching loss in Design B at high current, which is consistent with [3].
5 ImplicationIf this holds in service, Design B could reduce losses over a duty cycle dominated by high load.
6 LimitationHowever, a single prototype of each design was tested and no repeat measurements were made, so the difference at high load (at most 1.2 percentage points) may lie within measurement uncertainty. This limitation should be addressed in future work.
Do not skip move 6. A stated limitation does not weaken your thesis; it shows examiners that you understand what your data can and cannot support. Move 4 is a possible explanation unless you tested it.

Comparison and contrast

Comparing Series and Conditions

Report the direction, the size and the pattern of the gap, not only which value is higher.

FunctionLanguageExample (values from Figs. 1–3)

Curves meeting or separatingconverged · diverged · crossed · overtook · fell belowThe curves converged between 40% and 60% load, crossed at approximately 65%, and then diverged: Design B overtook Design A.

“Times higher” is ambiguous

“1.7 times higher than” can be read as 1.7 or as 2.7 times the original. Prefer 1.7 times as high as, 1.7 times that of, or give both values (148 and 87 Wh). Report the base of every percentage: “41% lower than Method 1”.

Compare like with like

“Method 3’s loss was lower than Method 1.” (compares a loss with a method). Better: “Method 3’s loss was lower than that of Method 1.” Use compared with or relative to, and keep the same unit on both sides.

Interpretation and evaluation language

Saying What the Pattern Means

Interpretation is a claim about meaning, not a measurement. Choose verbs that match your evidence.

FunctionLanguageExample (values from Figs. 1–3)
Interpret a patternthis suggests / indicates / implies that · these results point to · it appears thatThis suggests that Design B is better suited to high-load operation.
Offer a reasona possible explanation is · this may be attributed to · one reason may be · this may reflectThe shortfall at 14:00 may be attributed to intermittent cloud cover.
Link to evidenceconsistent with [3] · in line with [3] · in agreement with [3] · supports the finding of [3]The peak efficiency of Design B (96.7%) is consistent with the 96.2% reported in [3].
Disagree with evidencecontrary to [7] · in contrast to [7] · differs from [7] · at odds with [7]Whereas [7] reported a mean midday deviation of 4%, the deviation here was 6% at 12:00 and 25% at 14:00.
State an implicationtherefore · this implies · may / could + verb · has implications forDesign B may therefore be preferable for duty cycles dominated by high load.
Expected versus observedunexpectedly · lower than expected · was expected to… butOutput at 14:00 was lower than expected (4.1 compared with 5.5 kW simulated).
State a limitationhowever · a limitation of this study is that · should be interpreted with caution · may not generaliseA limitation of this study is that irradiance was not recorded.
Reporting verbs for the literature are usually in the past tense and describe what the authors did: [3] reported, found, observed, measured. Use [3] suggested only if the authors themselves hedged.
Calibrated claims

How Strong Is Your Claim?

Match the verb to the evidence. Overclaiming and underclaiming are both errors.

Strength scale (strongest first)demonstrates › indicates › suggests › may indicate
1 · Demonstrates / confirmsDirect, repeated, controlled evidence (or a tested difference).
The measurements demonstrate that output at 14:00 was below the simulated value (4.1 ± 0.9 against 5.5 kW).
2 · IndicatesConsistent evidence with little room for alternatives.
The close agreement at five of six times indicates that the model predicts output well at those times.
3 · SuggestsEvidence points one way, but other explanations remain.
This suggests that Design B is better suited to high load.
4 · May indicate / couldLimited evidence (single run, no repeats, untested reason).
The low 14:00 value may indicate transient cloud cover.
Avoid: proves, definitely, obviously, it is clear that, undoubtedly unless the evidence is mathematical. Hedging devices: modal verbs (may, could), lexical verbs (suggest, appear, tend), adverbs (possibly, probably, partly). This week you need hedging only for interpretation; Week 10 (Fact, Opinion, Evidence and Academic Neutrality) develops fact versus opinion and hedging in depth.
Correlation, causation and “significant”

Do Not Claim More Than the Design Allows

Causal language needs a causal design

  • Non-causal (safe): was associated with · coincided with · occurred together with · was accompanied by.
  • Causal (needs control): caused · led to · resulted in · was due to · reduced.
  • Fig. 2: low output and a large SD occurred together at 14:00. Without irradiance data we cannot say that cloud caused either; say may be attributed to.
  • Fig. 3: if all three methods ran on the same prototype under the same load profile, “Method 3 reduced the loss” is a reasonable causal claim. If conditions differed, write “was associated with a lower loss”.

When “significant” is allowed

  • Only after a statistical test, reported with the test, p value and n: “…was statistically significant (p < 0.05, n = 5).”
  • No test was run for Figs. 1–3, so write substantially, markedly or give the value.
  • Overlapping error bars (Method 2: 94 ± 7; Method 3: 87 ± 6 Wh) mean the 7 Wh difference is not clearly resolved.
  • Practical importance is separate from statistical significance: a 0.3 pp gap may be real but small.
Evaluating a figure and data quality

Ask What the Data Can Support

A good Discussion tells the reader how much weight each result can bear.

CheckQuestionLanguage frame (values from Figs. 1–3)
Sample size / repeatsHow many repeats? One prototype?Each bar is the mean of three repeated tests; with n = 3 the estimate of variability is itself uncertain. Fig. 1 reports a single prototype of each design.
Error barsWhat do they show (SD, SE, range)? Do they overlap?The error bars of Methods 2 and 3 overlap (94 ± 7 and 87 ± 6 Wh), so the 7 Wh difference cannot be regarded as resolved.
Outlier / anomalyWhich point does not fit? Is its spread unusual?The 14:00 value (4.1 ± 0.9 kW) is anomalous: its SD is three times that at 12:00 (0.3 kW).
Expected vs observedWhat did the model or literature predict? How large is the gap?Measured output was 25% below the simulation at 14:00 (4.1 compared with 5.5 kW), whereas differences at the other times did not exceed 0.3 kW.
Measurement limitsResolution, interval, instrument accuracy, missing variablesOutput was logged at two-hour intervals, so short-term fluctuations are not captured, and irradiance was not recorded.
Axis scaleDoes the axis start at zero?Note that the vertical axis in Fig. 1 starts at 84%, which visually exaggerates the gap between designs.
GeneralisabilityOne prototype, one site, one rating?These findings may not generalise to other converter ratings, because a single prototype of each design was tested.

Limitation formulas

  • A limitation of this study is that …
  • The results should be interpreted with caution because …
  • It was not possible to confirm whether … because …
  • Future work should … (to remove the limitation)

Good limitation vs weak limitation

Weak: “The experiment had some errors.”

Specific: “Only three repeats were performed per method, so the 7 Wh difference between Methods 2 and 3 should be interpreted with caution.”

Linking to literature

Comparing Your Results with a Cited Study

Give your value, the cited value, the difference, the conditions, and what the difference means.

RelationshipLanguageExample (placeholder reference values)
Agreementis consistent with [3] · in line with [3] · agrees closely with [3] · supports [3]The peak efficiency of Design B (96.7%) is consistent with the 96.2% reported in [3], a difference of 0.5 percentage points.
Partial agreementslightly smaller / larger than [5] · broadly in line with [5] · similar to butThe 36% loss reduction of Method 2 relative to Method 1 is slightly smaller than the 38% reported in [5].
Disagreementcontrary to [7] · whereas [7] · differs from [7]Whereas [7] reported a mean midday deviation of 4%, the deviation here was 6% at 12:00.

IEEE in-text citation

  • Numbered, in square brackets, in order of first appearance: [3]; several sources: [3], [5]; a range: [3]–[5].
  • Place the number before the punctuation: “…reported in [3].”
  • Begin a sentence with In [3], or Reference [3] reported; use the full name only when it adds meaning.
  • Give the full reference in the reference list. In class we use [3], [5], [7] as placeholders only.

A fair comparison checklist

  • Same quantity and unit (efficiency at rated load, not peak against rated).
  • Similar conditions or rating? If not, say so.
  • Report both values and the difference.
  • Give a possible reason for any difference, and hedge it.
  • Do not write “confirms” for a single close value; write is consistent with.
Grammar of discussion

Tense in the Discussion

Past tense for what happened; present tense for what it means and what is generally true.

You are…TenseExample
Reporting your own resultPast simpleDesign B reached 96.7% at 80% load.
Referring to a figurePresent simpleFig. 1 shows that the curves cross.
Interpreting your resultPresent simple + hedgeThis suggests that Design B is better suited to high load.
Stating a general truthPresent simpleSwitching loss increases with switching frequency.
Reporting a cited studyPast simple (one study); present perfect (a body of work)[3] reported 96.2%. Several studies have reported lower losses with soft switching.
Stating an implicationModal (may, could)Design B could reduce losses in high-load service.
Describing your limitationPast for what you did; present for its consequenceOnly three repeats were performed, so the result should be interpreted with caution.

A typical Discussion paragraph starts in the past (what you found), moves to the present (what it means) and ends with a modal (what it could imply).

One Finding, Many Ways to Write It

The same finding can be expressed with different grammar and a slightly different strength or register. Learn several structures so that your Discussion does not repeat one pattern.

“not possible to”It was not possible to confirm whether the 7 Wh difference between Methods 2 and 3 was real, because the error bars overlap.

Notes on strength

  • suggests, appears to and may therefore are similar in strength. indicates is stronger: do not swap it in when the evidence is a single run.
  • may be attributed to and a possible explanation is signal an untested reason. was caused by is a different, much stronger claim.
  • is consistent with says the values fit; it does not say the studies are identical. supports is a little stronger.

Notes on register

  • Impersonal passives (It may be inferred that…) are formal and common in theses; avoid stacking them.
  • Noun-phrase subjects (The efficiency advantage of Design B emerged…) suit a Discussion; verb forms suit an Abstract.
  • Keep the same values in every version. If you change the strength, you have changed the claim.

OptionalQuick Practice — Three Ways Optional · pre-class · 5 min

Situation (Fig. 3): Method 3 recorded 87 ± 6 Wh and Method 2 recorded 94 ± 7 Wh. Write the same limitation in three different structures, without using “significant”. Keep the values exact.

Show model answer
  • “cannot be regarded as”: The 7 Wh difference between Method 3 (87 ± 6 Wh) and Method 2 (94 ± 7 Wh) cannot be regarded as resolved, because the error bars overlap.
  • “however” + caution: Method 3 recorded a lower loss than Method 2 (by 7 Wh, or 7%); however, the overlapping error bars mean that this result should be interpreted with caution.
  • “A limitation is that”: A limitation of this study is that only three repeats were performed, so it is not possible to confirm that Method 3 outperformed Method 2.

Check: same values (87 ± 6, 94 ± 7, 7 Wh) · no “significant” · the claim is as strong as the evidence allows.

Core practice

From Description to Analysis

ScaffoldActivity A — Upgrade a Descriptive Paragraph 8 min

Descriptive paragraph (Fig. 1): Fig. 1 shows the efficiency of Design A and Design B at six loads. Design A was 91.0% at 10% load and 95.1% at 100% load. Design B was 86.5% at 10% load and 96.3% at 100% load. Design A was higher at low loads and Design B was higher at high loads.

Task: Rewrite it as 4–5 sentences that add (1) a quantified comparison, (2) the crossover, (3) a calibrated interpretation, (4) a possible reason or implication, and (5) a limitation. Keep Results sentences in the past and interpretation in the present.

Show model answer and checklist

Fig. 1 compares the efficiency of two converter designs at six loads. Design A was more efficient at low load, by 4.5 percentage points at 10% load, whereas Design B was more efficient above approximately 65% load, by 1.2 percentage points at 100% load. This suggests that Design B is better suited to converters operating mainly at high load, and a possible explanation is lower switching loss at high current. However, only one prototype of each design was tested, so the difference at high load should be interpreted with caution.

  • Comparison quantified in percentage points with load levels given.
  • Crossover located (approximately 65% load).
  • Interpretation uses a calibrated verb (suggests); the reason is hedged (a possible explanation is).
  • A specific limitation is stated; tense moves from past to present.

Research levelActivity D — Compare Your Result with a Literature Value 7 min

Data: Fig. 3: Method 2 reduced the daily loss from 148 to 94 Wh, a 36% reduction relative to Method 1. A (placeholder) reference [5] reported a 38% reduction for soft switching compared with hard switching.

Task: Write two sentences: (1) compare your 36% with the 38% in [5], giving the difference and a verb of agreement; (2) add a cautious reason for the difference and a limitation. Do not use “significant”.

Show model answer and checklist

The 36% reduction in daily energy loss achieved by Method 2 relative to Method 1 (148 to 94 Wh) is slightly smaller than the 38% reported in [5], and the two values are broadly in line with each other (a difference of 2 percentage points). The difference may reflect differences in load profile or component ratings, which cannot be checked from [5], and it lies within the variation indicated by the error bars (± 6–7 Wh) in Fig. 3.

  • Both values and their difference are given; the base of the percentage is stated.
  • Citation in IEEE style, number before punctuation.
  • The reason is hedged (may reflect); a limitation is stated; no “significant”, no “confirms”.

Research levelActivity E — Critique a Weak Discussion Paragraph 8 min

Fig. 2 shows that the simulation is wrong because the output at 14:00 was much lower. The significant difference proves that clouds reduced the output. This is a big problem and the model is bad. Our results are better than all previous studies. The system will work perfectly in all conditions.

Task: (1) Identify at least five problems and name each (overclaim, causal error, untested “significant”, vague wording, unsupported literature claim, missing limitation, tense). (2) Rewrite the paragraph in 4–5 sentences using the values of Fig. 2.

Show model answer and checklist
  • Faulty inference: a single anomalous time does not show that “the simulation is wrong”.
  • Vague: “much lower”, “a big problem”, “bad” give no values (4.1 against 5.5 kW).
  • Untested “significant”: no test was run; the SD at 14:00 is large.
  • Unsupported cause: “proves that clouds reduced” with no irradiance data.
  • Unsupported literature claim: “better than all previous studies” has no citation and no comparison.
  • Overclaim: “will work perfectly in all conditions”.
  • Missing limitation.

Rewrite: Fig. 2 shows close agreement between measured and simulated output at five of the six times (differences ≤ 0.3 kW). At 14:00, however, mean measured output (4.1 ± 0.9 kW) was 1.4 kW, or 25%, below the simulated 5.5 kW. This may be attributed to intermittent cloud cover, which is consistent with the large variation among the five days (SD = 0.9 kW), but irradiance was not recorded, so this explanation remains untested. The model therefore appears to predict output well at most times, but the 14:00 discrepancy should be investigated with logged irradiance data.

Checklist: five problems named · values and units · calibrated verbs · no “significant” or “proves” · limitation stated.

Choose one: (a) Fig. 1, (b) Fig. 2, (c) Fig. 3, or (d) a figure from your own thesis or pilot data (best option).

  1. Write 120–180 words in this order: observation → comparison → interpretation → explanation with evidence → implication → limitation.
  2. Include at least one quantified comparison (percentage points or percentage with base), one contrast linker, and two interpretations of different strength (e.g., suggests and may).
  3. Include one citation in IEEE style ([3], or a real source for option d).
  4. Use past tense for your results and present tense for interpretation.
  5. Do not use “significant” without a test, and do not state an untested cause as a fact.

Peer review (4 min): swap, score with the rubric in the Homework section, tick the quick checks in the peer table, and give one revision suggestion.

Show model answer (Fig. 3) and checklist

Daily energy loss fell from 148 ± 6 Wh for Method 1 to 94 ± 7 Wh for Method 2 and 87 ± 6 Wh for Method 3 (Fig. 3). Method 3 outperformed Method 1 by 61 Wh, a 41% reduction, whereas the 7 Wh difference between Methods 2 and 3 was small compared with the error bars. This suggests that soft switching accounts for most of the improvement, and the proposed control strategy may add a further, smaller gain. The 36% reduction of Method 2 is consistent with the 38% reported in [5]. A possible explanation is reduced switching loss. If confirmed, the proposed method could lower annual energy loss. However, only three repeats were performed, so the difference between Methods 2 and 3 should be interpreted with caution.

  • Six moves present; Results sentences in the past, interpretation in the present.
  • Two interpretations of different strength (suggests, may); one cited comparison; a specific limitation.
  • Quantities correct: 61 Wh = 148 − 87; 61/148 = 41%; 54/148 = 36%.
