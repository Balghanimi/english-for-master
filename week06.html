From Describing Data to Discussing It

Connection to Week 5: Week 5 taught you to answer “What does the data show?” using accurate values, trends and comparisons. Week 6 asks “What does it mean, and how strong is the evidence?” You will compare conditions, interpret findings, connect them to research, and state implications and limitations. Match every claim to its evidence.
Why this matters for your research: The Results section reports findings. The Discussion explains their meaning and limits. Unsupported claims and missing limitations weaken academic writing. Bring your Week 5 figure, caption, paragraph and revision log to improve them in the homework.

Warm-up — What Is Missing from This Description?

Before learning each move, try to improve one descriptive paragraph. Use the six-move path as a checklist.

Situation: An MSc student describes Fig. 1 (efficiency of two converter designs; the data are in the Figures section) in a draft Results chapter.

Fig. 1 shows the efficiency of Design A and Design B at six loads. Design A was 91.0% at 10% load and 95.1% at 100% load. Design B was 86.5% at 10% load and 96.3% at 100% load. Design A was higher at low loads and Design B was higher at high loads.

Placeholder references: [3], [5] and [7] in this lesson are hypothetical placeholders for classroom practice. They are not real papers and their values are not real findings.

ScaffoldStep 1 · Diagnose 3 min

The description is accurate. Individually (2 min), list four things a supervisor would still ask (for example: So what? Where do they cross? Why? How sure are you?), then compare in pairs (1 min).

Show model diagnosis
  • No quantified comparison: “higher” has no size (4.5 percentage points at 10% load; 1.2 percentage points at 100% load).
  • No crossover: the reader is not told where the order reverses (between 60% and 80% load, about 66% by linear interpolation).
  • No interpretation: what does the pattern imply for use? (e.g., This may suggest that Design B is better suited to high-load operation.)
  • No explanation, literature link or limitation: a reason, a comparison with [3], and a statement of what was tested (one prototype per design).

ScaffoldStep 2 · Activity A: upgrade the paragraph 7 min + 2 min comparison

Task: Write 4–5 sentences. Include (1) a numerical comparison, (2) the crossover, (3) a cautious interpretation, (4) a possible reason or implication, and (5) a limitation. Use past tense for findings and present tense for interpretation.

Show model answer and checklist

Fig. 1 compares the efficiency of two converter designs at six loads. Design A was more efficient at low load, by 4.5 percentage points at 10% load, whereas Design B was more efficient at 80% and 100% load, by 0.7 and 1.2 percentage points; the curves crossed between 60% and 80% load (about 66% by linear interpolation). This may suggest that Design B is better suited to converters operating mainly at high load, and a possible explanation is lower switching loss at high current. However, only one prototype of each design was tested, so the difference at high load should be interpreted with caution.

  • Comparison quantified in percentage points with load levels given.
  • Crossover located (between 60% and 80% load).
  • Interpretation uses a calibrated verb (may suggest: one prototype, no repeats); the reason is hedged (a possible explanation is).
  • A specific limitation is stated; tense moves from past to present.
Core lesson · The writing blueprint · Results and Discussion

The Six Moves: From Reporting to Discussion

In Results, report observations and comparisons (Moves 1–2). In Discussion, add meaning, explanation, implications and limits (Moves 3–6).

Keep this distinction: Results = what was measured (Moves 1–2). Discussion = what it means, why it may happen, why it matters and what remains uncertain (Moves 3–6). Use the same six moves when a thesis combines Results and Discussion.

Move 2 of 6 Results

Comparison

Which value is higher? By how much?Does the difference change?

Model sentence · Fig. 1

Design A was more efficient at low loads: by 4.5 percentage points at 10%. However, Design B was higher by 0.7 points at 80% load and 1.2 at 100%. The curves crossed between 60% and 80% load (about 66% by linear interpolation).

State which is higher or lower, by how much, and how the gap changes. Do not invent a reason yet.

Comparison language
FunctionLanguageExample (values from Figs. 1–3)
Contrast two valueswhereas · while · in contrast · by contrast · on the other handDesign A was more efficient at 10% load (91.0%), whereas Design B was more efficient at 100% load (96.3%).
Compare with a quantityN pp higher than · N% lower than · N times as high as · compared with · relative toCompared with Method 1, Method 3 lost 61 Wh less energy per day (87 against 148 Wh), i.e. 41% less relative to Method 1.
RatioN times as high as · N times that of · a factor of NMethod 1’s daily loss was 1.7 times that of Method 3 (148 and 87 Wh).
Rank / outperform (evaluative)outperformed · exceeded · surpassed · the highest / lowest · ranked firstMethod 3 had the lowest mean loss (87 Wh) and outperformed Method 1; its 7 Wh advantage over Method 2 lies within the error bars.
Gap patternthe gap widened / narrowed / closed / reversedThe efficiency gap narrowed from 4.5 pp at 10% load to 0.3 pp at 60% load.
Curves meeting or separatingconverged · diverged · crossed · overtook · fell belowThe curves converged between 40% and 60% load, crossed between 60% and 80% load (about 66% by linear interpolation), and then diverged: Design B overtook Design A.
Similaritysimilar · comparable · closely matched · in agreement withMeasured and simulated output were closely matched at five of six times (differences ≤ 0.3 kW).

“Outperformed” means better, not simply lower. Use it only if the difference is clear despite the error bars. Otherwise, write was lower than.

“Times higher” is ambiguous

“1.7 times higher than” is ambiguous: it may mean 1.7 or 2.7 times the original. Prefer 1.7 times as high as, 1.7 times that of, or give both values (148 and 87 Wh). Report the base of every percentage: “41% lower than Method 1”.

Compare like with like

Do not compare a measurement with a method. Write: “Method 3’s loss was lower than that of Method 1.” Use compared with or relative to, and keep the same unit on both sides.

Move 4 of 6 Discussion

Explanation with Evidence

What might explain the pattern?Support the reason with data or research.

Model sentence · Fig. 1

A possible explanation is lower switching loss in Design B at high current, as [3] also proposes for designs of this type.

Offer a possible reason, then support or compare it with experimental evidence or published research. Do not claim an untested cause as fact.

First explain, then support: Say what might cause the pattern (for example, lower switching loss), then examine whether your measurements or another study support this explanation. Sources [3], [5] and [7] below are hypothetical classroom placeholders, not real papers.

Classroom agreement rule: Compare the gap with the uncertainty of your own measurement (e.g., 1 SD in the same unit). If the gap is smaller, write consistent with; if larger, write differs from. If no uncertainty is given (Fig. 1), state this and write similar to. This is a guide, not a statistical test.

IEEE in-text citation

  • Numbered, in square brackets, in order of first appearance: [3]; several sources: [3], [5]; a range: [3]–[5].
  • Place the number before the punctuation: “…reported in [3].”
  • Begin a sentence with In [3], or Reference [3] reported; use the full name only when it adds meaning.
  • Give the full reference in the reference list. In class we use [3], [5] and [7] as hypothetical placeholders only.

A fair comparison checklist

  • Same quantity and unit (efficiency at rated load, not peak against rated).
  • Similar conditions or rating? If not, say so.
  • Report both values and the difference.
  • Give a possible reason for any difference, and hedge it.
  • Do not write “confirms” for a single close value; write is consistent with or is similar to.
Use past-tense reporting verbs for one study: Reference [3] reported, found, observed, measured. Use suggested when the authors used cautious language. For limitations, continue to Move 6.

Move 5 of 6 Discussion

Implication

Why does the finding matter for an engineering system or application?

Model sentence · Fig. 1

If this holds in service, Design B could reduce losses over a duty cycle dominated by high load.

Connect the finding to a possible engineering use. Say what it could mean in practice without promising more than the tests show.

Detailed explanation: Move 5 — Explain Why the Finding MattersOptional
FunctionLanguageEngineering example
State an implicationtherefore · this implies · may / could + verb · has implications forDesign B may therefore be preferable for duty cycles dominated by high load.

Results → implication

Measured fact: Design B had higher efficiency at 80% and 100% load in the illustrative test.

Possible use: Design B could reduce losses in applications that operate mainly at high load.

Do not overclaim

Avoid: Design B will save energy in every converter.

Better: If the measured trend also occurs under service conditions, Design B may reduce energy losses.

Move 5 asks: So what? Move 6 asks the final question: What do the current measurements not tell us?

Move 6 of 6 Discussion

Limitation

What can the data NOT establish?Identify uncertainty or missing tests.

Model sentence · Fig. 1

However, only one prototype per design was tested, with no repeat measurements. The high-load difference (at most 1.2 percentage points) may be within measurement uncertainty and should be interpreted with caution.

Check repeats, error bars, unusual values and missing measurements. Write one specific limitation supported by the study design.

Always include a limitation. It shows what the evidence cannot establish. In move 4, a reason is still possible unless you tested it.
Detailed explanation: Move 6 — Evaluate the Evidence and State Its LimitsOptional
Compare predicted and measured values: At 14:00, measured output (2.8 kW) was lower than expected from the simulation (4.0 kW). Useful expressions include unexpectedly and was expected to … but. The gap should be investigated; it does not prove a specific cause.
Checks for evaluating a figure
CheckQuestionLanguage frame (values from Figs. 1–3)
Sample size / repeatsHow many repeats? One prototype?Each bar is the mean of three repeated tests; with n = 3 the estimate of variability is itself uncertain. Fig. 1 reports a single prototype of each design.
Error barsWhat do they show (SD, SE, range)? Do they overlap?The SD bars of Methods 2 and 3 overlap (94 ± 7 and 87 ± 6 Wh), so they do not resolve the 7 Wh difference; an overlap is not a significance test.
Outlier / anomalyWhich point does not fit? Is its spread unusual?The 14:00 value (2.8 ± 0.9 kW) is anomalous: its SD is three times that at 12:00 (0.3 kW).
Expected vs observedWhat did the model or literature predict? How large is the gap?Measured output was 30% below the simulation at 14:00 (2.8 compared with 4.0 kW), whereas differences at the other times did not exceed 0.3 kW and were no larger than the SD.
Measurement limitsResolution, interval, instrument accuracy, missing variablesOutput was logged at two-hour intervals, so short-term fluctuations are not captured, and irradiance was not recorded.
Axis scaleDoes the axis start at zero?Note that the vertical axis in Fig. 1 starts at 84%, which visually exaggerates the gap between designs.
GeneralisabilityOne prototype, one site, one rating?These findings may not generalise to other converter ratings, because a single prototype of each design was tested.

Limitation formulas

  • A limitation of this study is that …
  • The results should be interpreted with caution because …
  • It was not possible to confirm whether … because …
  • Future work should … (to remove the limitation)

Good limitation vs weak limitation

Weak: “The experiment had some errors.”

Specific: “Only three repeats were performed per method, so the 7 Wh difference between Methods 2 and 3 should be interpreted with caution.”

Writing check · Use with all six moves

Writing Check — Match Your Claim to the Evidence

This is NOT a seventh move. Check that each factual statement, interpretation and possible explanation is no stronger or weaker than the evidence supports.

Evidence bands A–C (a writing check, not the six moves)A · Observed fact › B · Supported inference › C · Tentative or untested explanation
A · Observed factDirect measurements. Use: was, reached, was lower than, fell; Fig. 1 shows…. No hedge is needed.
Output at 12:00 was 4.7 ± 0.3 kW, against 5.0 kW simulated (Fig. 2).
B · Supported inferenceAn interpretation supported by repeats, a clear pattern or independent evidence. Use: suggests, indicates, is consistent with.
The close agreement at five of six times indicates that the model predicts output well at those times (n = 5 days).
C · Tentative or untested explanationLimited evidence or an untested reason. Use: may, could, may suggest, may indicate, may be attributed to, a possible explanation is.
This may suggest that Design B is better suited to high load; only one prototype of each design was tested.

How to use the bands

  • Decide first what you assert, then check that the evidence can carry that band.
  • One prototype and no repeats (Fig. 1): the observation stays in band A, but any interpretation of it stays in band C (may suggest, may indicate).
  • Underclaiming is also an error: a value that was measured is band A. Design B might possibly have been a little more efficient at 100% load hedges a number that is in the table.

A classroom convention

These bands are a classroom guide, not a universal rule. Suggests and indicates can overlap. Follow your supervisor and target journal.

Avoid: proves, definitely, obviously, it is clear that, undoubtedly unless the evidence is mathematical, and treat demonstrates and confirms with care: they need controlled, repeated or independently verified evidence. Hedging devices: modal verbs (may, could), lexical verbs (suggest, appear, tend), adverbs (possibly, probably, partly). This week you need hedging only for interpretation; Week 10 (Fact, Opinion, Evidence and Academic Neutrality) develops fact versus opinion and hedging in depth.
Correlation, causation and “significant”

Do Not Claim More Than the Design Allows

Causal language needs a causal design

  • Non-causal (safe): was associated with · coincided with · occurred together with · was accompanied by.
  • Causal (needs control): caused · led to · resulted in · was due to · reduced.
  • Fig. 2: low output and a large SD occurred together at 14:00. Say that they occurred together; do not state a cause without irradiance data (Activity E shows how to word an untested reason).
  • Fig. 3: if all three methods ran on the same prototype under the same load profile, and the order of the tests was randomised, “Method 3 reduced the loss” is a reasonable causal claim. If conditions differed, write “was associated with a lower loss”.

When “significant” is allowed

  • Only after a statistical test, reported with the test, the p value and n: “…was statistically significant (p < 0.05, n = 5).”
  • No statistical test was run for Figs. 1–3. Describe a large gap using substantially or markedly, or simply give the value.
  • Overlapping SD bars (Method 2: 94 ± 7; Method 3: 87 ± 6 Wh) do not resolve the 7 Wh difference. An overlap is not a significance test; with only three repeats, these data cannot tell the two methods apart.
  • Practical importance is separate from statistical significance: a 0.3 pp gap may be real but small.

Notes on register

  • Impersonal passives (It may be inferred that…) are formal and common in theses; avoid stacking them.
  • Noun-phrase subjects (The efficiency advantage of Design B emerged…) suit a Discussion; verb forms suit an Abstract.
  • Keep the same values in every version. If you change the strength band, you have changed the claim.

  6. Design A was more efficient at low load, by 4.5 percentage points at 10% load, whereas Design B was more efficient at 80% and 100% load (by 0.7 and 1.2 percentage points); the curves crossed between 60% and 80% load (about 66% by linear interpolation).

Part 2 · Critique (7 min).

Fig. 2 shows that the simulation is wrong because the output at 14:00 was much lower. The significant difference proves that clouds reduced the output. This is a big problem and the model is bad. Our results are better than all previous studies. The system will work perfectly in all conditions.

Task: (1) Identify at least three problems and name each (overclaim, causal error, untested “significant”, vague wording, unsupported literature claim, missing limitation). (2) Rewrite the first two sentences using the values of Fig. 2.

Show model answer and checklist

Part 1: (i) 4 · (ii) 1 · (iii) 6 · (iv) 3 · (v) 5 · (vi) 2.

  • Faulty inference: a single anomalous time does not show that “the simulation is wrong”.
  • Vague: “much lower”, “a big problem”, “bad” give no values (2.8 against 4.0 kW).
  • Untested “significant”: no test was run; the SD at 14:00 is large.
  • Unsupported cause: “proves that clouds reduced” with no irradiance data.
  • Unsupported literature claim: “better than all previous studies” has no citation and no comparison.
  • Overclaim: “will work perfectly in all conditions”.
  • Missing limitation.

Rewrite of the first two sentences: At 14:00, mean measured output (2.8 ± 0.9 kW) was 1.2 kW, or 30%, below the simulated 4.0 kW, whereas the two agreed closely at the other five times (Fig. 2). This may be attributed to intermittent cloud cover, but irradiance was not recorded, so the explanation remains untested.

Extension (full paragraph): add that the model appears to predict output well at most times and that the 14:00 discrepancy should be investigated with logged irradiance data.

Checklist: at least three problems named · values and units · calibrated verbs · no “significant” or “proves” · limitation stated.

The 36% lower loss of Method 2 relative to Method 1 (148 to 94 Wh) is slightly smaller than the 38% reported in [5]; the difference of 2 percentage points is about 3 Wh on a 148 Wh base, smaller than the SDs in Fig. 3 (6 and 7 Wh), so the values are consistent with each other, although [5] reports no uncertainty.
